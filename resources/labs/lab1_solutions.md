# Solutions

**Q1.** The alpha elements track hydrostatic O/Ne/Si-burning in the core; their absolute yield still drops somewhat at high Z (net O: ~0.275 Msun at Z=0.5 Zsun vs ~0.100 Msun at Z=3 Zsun, a factor of ~2.7), but the odd-Z elements (Na, Al, P, Cl, K, Sc) are far more metallicity-*sensitive* in the way that matters here: their production requires a neutron excess set by the star's initial C, N, O, Ne content (via 22Ne, 25Mg, 26Mg), so more initial metals directly means more odd-Z production -- the metal-rich model makes roughly 2x more odd-Z yield than the metal-poor one. This is the classic odd-even effect strengthening with metallicity.

**Q2.** The weak s-process (Ga-Rb-Sr-Y-Zr, seeded by 22Ne(alpha,n)25Mg during core He/C burning) is the other strongly metallicity-dependent group: essentially *absent* in the metal-poor net yield (net sum ~2e-6 Msun, consistent with zero) but ~15x larger at high metallicity. It stays concentrated in Ga-Rb rather than reaching Sr-Y-Zr.

Switching `YIELD_COL` to `"final [Msun]"` flips the Fe story: the *final* ejecta Fe mass is actually *larger* at high Z (~0.058 Msun) than at low Z (~0.025 Msun), even though the *net* Fe yield runs the other way (~0.0035 vs ~0.016 Msun). Most of the "extra" Fe an observer would measure in the high-Z ejecta was already present at birth (inherited from the star's Z=3 Zsun natal composition), not newly made.

**Q3.** Rotation would most strongly boost the odd-Z and weak-s groups, since rotational mixing dredges extra 14N and 22Ne into the He-burning region (the ingredients Q1/Q2 show are otherwise rare at low metallicity). But the bigger enhancement would show up in the **sub-solar**, not the super-solar. The reason is mass loss: stellar wind rates scale strongly with metallicity, so the super-solar progenitor loses its H-rich envelope (and the angular momentum stored in it) much earlier in its evolution, cutting off rotational mixing before it can efficiently feed 14N/22Ne into the core-adjacent burning shells. The metal-poor progenitor keeps both its envelope and its spin for longer, giving rotation time to act as a primary channel (see Roberti, Limongi & Chieffi 2024, ApJS 271, 21).

**Q4.** The light trans-Fe elements Zn-Ge-Se-Sr-Y-Zr (Z~30-40) diverge the most. The ecSN shows a clear bump there (90Zr alone is 1.2e-4 Msun, ~5% of its own 56Fe mass) that neither Type II model reaches at a comparable level. The metal-rich Type II's weak-s signature stays confined to Ga-Rb (Z=31-37) and never builds up a comparable Sr-Y-Zr peak, while the metal-poor Type II has essentially none of it (see the Type II section above).

**Q5.** Explosive. The ecSN progenitor is only 8.8 Msun and ejects just ~1.4e-2 Msun total (vs ~13-14 Msun for the Type II models) -- almost the whole ejecta is innermost core material, exposed to the intense neutrino flux from the newly formed proto-neutron star during collapse and bounce. That neutrino irradiation drives Ye below 0.5 in those innermost layers and builds the Zn-Zr bump.

**Q6.** The Fe-peak / alpha-freeze-out elements (Si-S-Ar-Ca-Fe) track most closely in shape. Both show the same even>odd alternation with a peak at Fe, even though the ecSN's absolute masses sit 2-3 orders of magnitude lower. That shape agreement despite the huge scale difference is a signature of shared physics: explosive Si burning and alpha-rich freeze-out near the mass cut, just acting on a far smaller ejected mass in the ecSN.

**Q7.** Type II ejects more O than Fe (final O ~0.325 Msun vs final Fe ~0.025 Msun at Z=0.5 Zsun -- an O/Fe mass ratio of ~13), while Type Ia ejects more Fe than O (~0.81 Msun Fe vs ~0.056 Msun O -- an O/Fe ratio of ~0.07, roughly 190x smaller). Type II supernovae are therefore the Galaxy's dominant source of O (and the other alpha elements) while Type Ia are the dominant source of Fe. Because Type II progenitors are short-lived (~Myr) and enrich a galaxy with alpha elements almost immediately, while Type Ia have a long delay time (their WD progenitors can take up to a Gyr to reach ignition), early galactic gas is alpha-enhanced ([alpha/Fe] > 0); once Type Ia start contributing in bulk, Fe floods in faster than the alpha elements and [alpha/Fe] declines. This gives the resulting "knee" in [alpha/Fe] vs [Fe/H].

**Q8.** No -- the *elemental* Fe yield is nearly metallicity-independent (~0.81 Msun at Z=0.5 Zsun vs ~0.77 Msun at Z=3 Zsun, only a ~5% difference), but Mn (Z=25) rises with progenitor metallicity (~0.0095 -> ~0.0138 Msun, a ~45% increase). A higher-metallicity (more neutron-rich) white dwarf progenitor shifts burning from radioactive, neutron-poor 56Ni towards directly-synthesised, neutron-rich stable isotopes like 55Mn and 54Fe (54Fe/56Fe roughly doubles, 0.14 -> 0.24). This is the Type Ia manganese clock used observationally to probe the metallicity distribution of the SN Ia progenitor population.

**Q9.** Look at the sign of each yield, not just the size of the nitrogen one. In the metal-poor model, both C (+0.0041 Msun) and N (+0.0401 Msun) are net positive. This suggests the star is freshly manufacturing new carbon (via He-shell burning + third dredge-up) and shipping some of it to the surface as C while hot bottom burning converts the rest to N. In the metal-rich model, C is net *negative* (-0.0109 Msun) while N is still net positive (+0.0303 Msun). The carbon reservoir is shrinking while nitrogen grows, the fingerprint of hot bottom burning's CN cycle converting carbon into nitrogen faster than dredge-up can replace it. 

**Q10.** AGB stars are not major producers of the dominant oxygen isotope, 16O. It's essentially untouched by AGB burning, and can even be mildly destroyed via the ON cycle at the base of a strong HBB. There is a secondary channel, though: some 17O is made during hydrogen burning, when 16O(p,gamma)17F(beta+,nu)17O manages to dominate over the destruction channels 17O(p,gamma)18F and 17O(p,alpha)14N (Marigo 2001). 

**Q11.** The standard trap here is judging s- vs r-process from Eu alone. Both stars have strongly enhanced Eu ([Eu/Fe]=+1.44 for CS 29497-030, +1.64 for CS 22892-052). The actual discriminator is the **Ba/Eu ratio**: CS 29497-030 has [Ba/Fe]=+2.17 against [Eu/Fe]=+1.44, giving [Ba/Eu]=+0.73 (Ba >> Eu -- s-process); CS 22892-052 has [Ba/Fe]=+0.99 against [Eu/Fe]=+1.64, giving [Ba/Eu]=-0.65 (Eu >> Ba -- r-process). 

Pb is where the two curves' shapes diverge most dramatically. CS 29497-030 (CEMP-s) keeps climbing all the way to Pb -- [Ba/Fe]=+2.17 rises further to [Pb/Fe]=+3.55, more than a full 1 dex jump. CS 22892-052 (CEMP-r) peaks in the rare-earth/third r-process-peak region (Dy, Ir, Pt all around [X/Fe]~1.6-1.7) and turns back down by Pb ([Pb/Fe]=+1.20). This is the lead star signature of low-metallicity AGB s-process nucleosynthesis. At [Fe/H]=-2.70 there are very few Fe seeds available, so each seed that captures a neutron has on average far more free neutrons per seed to absorb than it would at solar metallicity. That pushes the s-process flow past the second peak (Ba-La-Ce) and piles material up disproportionately at the third peak (Pb). 

**Q12.** Mass transfer dilutes the AGB companion's s-process-rich wind into the unprocessed envelope of the (much less evolved) low-mass star we actually observe. A single-star model tells you what the donor produced internally, so the dilution factor (which depends on the accretor's own structure and mixing depth) is a free parameter no single-star yield table can give you. Binary evolution might also change the donor's own nucleosynthesis -- e.g. Roche-lobe overflow can truncate its AGB evolution early, cutting off the number of thermal pulses/dredge-up episodes it experiences.

**Q13.** The presence or absence of Tc (technetium) lines. Tc has no stable isotopes; its longest-lived, 99Tc, has a half-life of only ~2.1e5 years. If a CEMP-s star currently shows Tc lines, it must be intrinsic. CS 29497-030 shows no Tc (this doesn't guarantee extrinsic, it just means we cannot confirm it is intrinsic).
