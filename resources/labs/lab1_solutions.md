# Solutions

**Q1.** The alpha elements track hydrostatic O/Ne/Si-burning in the core; their absolute yield still drops somewhat at high Z (net O: ~0.275 Msun at Z=0.5 Zsun vs ~0.100 Msun at Z=3 Zsun, a factor of ~2.7), but the odd-Z elements (Na, Al, P, Cl, K, Sc) are far more metallicity-*sensitive* in the way that matters here: their production requires a neutron excess set by the star's initial C, N, O, Ne content (via 22Ne, 25Mg, 26Mg), so more initial metals directly means more odd-Z production -- the metal-rich model makes roughly 2x more odd-Z yield than the metal-poor one. This is the classic odd-even effect strengthening with metallicity.

**Q2.** The weak s-process (Ga-Rb-Sr-Y-Zr, seeded by 22Ne(alpha,n)25Mg during core He/C burning) is the other strongly metallicity-dependent group: essentially *absent* in the metal-poor net yield (net sum ~2e-6 Msun, consistent with zero) but ~15x larger at high metallicity. It stays concentrated in Ga-Rb rather than reaching Sr-Y-Zr, though -- so it's not "weak-s gets stronger and reaches further with metallicity," it's closer to "absent at low Z, Ga-Rb only once metal-rich."

Switching `YIELD_COL` to `"final [Msun]"` flips the Fe story: the *final* ejecta Fe mass is actually *larger* at high Z (~0.058 Msun) than at low Z (~0.025 Msun), even though the *net* Fe yield runs the other way (~0.0035 vs ~0.016 Msun). Most of the "extra" Fe an observer would measure in the high-Z ejecta was already present at birth (inherited from the star's Z=3 Zsun natal composition), not newly made -- final abundance is not the same thing as net yield, and reading the wrong one would lead you to conclude the metal-rich star is the *better* Fe producer when it's actually the worse one.

**Q3.** Rotation would most strongly boost the odd-Z and weak-s groups, since rotational mixing dredges extra 14N and 22Ne into the He-burning region -- exactly the ingredients Q1/Q2 show are otherwise scarce at low metallicity. But the *bigger* enhancement would show up in the **sub-solar**, not the super-solar, model -- which is counter-intuitive if your instinct is "more metals in, more rotational mixing products out." The reason is mass loss: stellar wind rates scale strongly with metallicity, so the super-solar progenitor loses its H-rich envelope (and the angular momentum stored in it) much earlier in its evolution, cutting off rotational mixing before it can efficiently feed 14N/22Ne into the core-adjacent burning shells. The metal-poor progenitor keeps both its envelope and its spin for longer, giving rotation time to act as a genuine primary channel. This is exactly the trend Roberti, Limongi & Chieffi (2024, ApJS 271, 21) find when they extend these rotating massive-star grids to super-solar metallicity ([Fe/H]=0.3): the neutron-capture (weak-s) yields relative to oxygen are *higher* in the metal-rich models than the solar ones when rotation is switched off, but that ordering *reverses* once rotation is switched on -- the enhanced mass loss at high Z robs the super-solar rotating models of the extra weak-s boost that the same rotation gives a metal-poor star. This is also the physical basis of the "spinstar" picture for early Galactic chemical evolution, where rotating, very metal-poor massive stars are invoked as a primary nitrogen/weak-s source precisely because they don't lose this rotational advantage to strong winds the way metal-rich stars do.

**Q4.** The light trans-Fe elements Zn-Ge-Se-Sr-Y-Zr (Z~30-40) diverge the most: the ecSN shows a clear bump there (90Zr alone is 1.2e-4 Msun, ~5% of its own 56Fe mass) that neither Type II model reaches at a comparable level -- the metal-rich Type II's weak-s signature stays confined to Ga-Rb (Z=31-37) and never builds up a comparable Sr-Y-Zr peak, while the metal-poor Type II has essentially none of it (see the Type II section above).

**Q5.** Explosive. The ecSN progenitor is only 8.8 Msun and ejects just ~1.4e-2 Msun total (vs ~13-14 Msun for the Type II models) -- almost the whole ejecta is innermost core material, exposed to the intense neutrino flux from the newly formed proto-neutron star during collapse and bounce. That neutrino irradiation drives Ye below 0.5 in those innermost layers, and it's exactly that neutron excess, imprinted explosively within seconds, that builds the Zn-Zr bump -- a genuinely distinct, "weak r-process-like" fingerprint that hydrostatic shell burning (which built the Type II weak-s pattern over millennia pre-explosion) cannot produce.

**Q6.** The Fe-peak / alpha-freeze-out elements (Si-S-Ar-Ca-Fe) track most closely in shape -- both show the same even>odd alternation with a peak at Fe -- even though the ecSN's absolute masses sit 2-3 orders of magnitude lower. That shape agreement despite the huge scale difference is a signature of shared physics: explosive Si burning and alpha-rich freeze-out near the mass cut, just acting on a far smaller ejected mass in the ecSN.

**Q7.** Type II ejects far more O than Fe (final O ~0.325 Msun vs final Fe ~0.025 Msun at Z=0.5 Zsun -- an O/Fe mass ratio of ~13), while Type Ia ejects far more Fe than O (~0.81 Msun Fe vs ~0.056 Msun O -- an O/Fe ratio of ~0.07, roughly 190x smaller). Type II supernovae are therefore the Galaxy's dominant source of O (and the other alpha elements); Type Ia are the dominant source of Fe. Because Type II progenitors are short-lived (~Myr) and enrich a galaxy with alpha elements almost immediately, while Type Ia have a long delay time (their WD progenitors can take up to a Gyr to reach ignition), early galactic gas is alpha-enhanced ([alpha/Fe] > 0); once Type Ia start contributing in bulk, Fe floods in faster than the alpha elements and [alpha/Fe] declines. The resulting "knee" in [alpha/Fe] vs [Fe/H] is a direct fossil record of this delay-time difference.

**Q8.** No -- the *elemental* Fe yield is nearly metallicity-independent (~0.81 Msun at Z=0.5 Zsun vs ~0.77 Msun at Z=3 Zsun, only a ~5% difference, invisible on a log-y plot spanning many orders of magnitude), but that flatness hides a real effect one element over: Mn (Z=25) rises measurably with progenitor metallicity (~0.0095 -> ~0.0138 Msun, a ~45% increase). A higher-metallicity (more neutron-rich) white dwarf progenitor shifts burning from radioactive, neutron-poor 56Ni towards directly-synthesised, neutron-rich stable isotopes like 55Mn and 54Fe (54Fe/56Fe roughly doubles, 0.14 -> 0.24 -- an isotope ratio this *elemental* plot can't show at all). This Mn/Fe trend is exactly the "Type Ia manganese clock" used observationally to probe the metallicity distribution of the SN Ia progenitor population -- a good reminder that a flat-looking element can still be hiding a real trend in its isotopes or its neighbours.

**Q9.** No -- the Leung & Nomoto grid reports no yields past Zn (Z=30) at all, and even within the Fe-peak there's no sign of the strong neutron-rich bump seen in the ecSN. A near-Chandrasekhar C/O deflagration burns at Ye much closer to 0.5 (far less neutron-rich) than a collapsing degenerate core exposed to neutrino irradiation, because there's no proto-neutron star pumping neutrinos into the ejecta and no core-collapse electron captures at these densities -- so Type Ia has neither the neutron excess nor the free neutrons needed to run any s- or r-process at all.

**Q10.** Look at the sign of each yield, not just the size of the nitrogen one. In the metal-poor model, *both* C (+0.0041 Msun) and N (+0.0401 Msun) are net *positive* -- the star is a genuine primary source, freshly manufacturing new carbon (via He-shell burning + third dredge-up) and shipping some of it to the surface as C while hot bottom burning converts the rest to N. Both elements come out ahead: the star adds brand-new C *and* N to the ISM. In the metal-rich model, C is net *negative* (-0.0109 Msun) while N is still net positive (+0.0303 Msun) -- the carbon reservoir is shrinking while nitrogen grows, the fingerprint of hot bottom burning's CN cycle converting carbon into nitrogen faster than dredge-up can replace it. So even though the two models make a similar *quantity* of nitrogen, they get there in qualitatively different ways: the metal-poor star is contributing genuinely new (primary) metal mass to Galactic chemical evolution, while the metal-rich star behaves more like a secondary process, mostly reshuffling its own carbon into nitrogen rather than manufacturing new material from scratch. (This is a looser use of "primary/secondary" than the strict textbook definition -- which is about whether a yield scales with the star's initial metal content -- but the same underlying physics, hot bottom burning acting on dredged-up carbon, explains both framings.)

**Q11.** AGB stars are not major producers of the dominant oxygen isotope, 16O -- it's essentially untouched by AGB burning, and can even be mildly destroyed via the ON cycle at the base of a strong HBB. There is a secondary channel, though: some 17O *is* made during hydrogen burning, when 16O(p,gamma)17F(beta+,nu)17O manages to dominate over the destruction channels 17O(p,gamma)18F and 17O(p,alpha)14N (Marigo 2001) -- it's a minor isotope, nowhere near as abundant as 16O, but it is a genuine small primary-like contribution. The *elemental* O sign flip you see here (net +0.0076 Msun at low Z vs -0.0100 Msun at high Z) is dominated by what happens to the much more abundant 16O, not by this small 17O channel: at low metallicity, third dredge-up wins and brings up a little freshly-made 16O along with the carbon, for a net gain; at high metallicity HBB is comparatively more efficient at this mass and starts eating into the 16O reservoir via the ON cycle, for a net loss that outweighs the small 17O gain. Either way the effect is tiny next to Type II's O yield (~0.01 Msun here vs ~0.3-0.4 Msun there) -- oxygen (specifically 16O) is a hydrostatic-massive-star signature, not an AGB one.

**Q12.** The standard trap here is judging s- vs r-process from Eu alone: both stars have strongly enhanced Eu ([Eu/Fe]=+1.44 for CS 29497-030, +1.64 for CS 22892-052), so "high Eu -> r-process" would call *both* of these r-process stars. But the AGB s-process also makes real amounts of Eu -- just proportionally far less of it than Ba. The actual discriminator is the **Ba/Eu ratio**: CS 29497-030 has [Ba/Fe]=+2.17 against [Eu/Fe]=+1.44, giving [Ba/Eu]=+0.73 (Ba >> Eu -- s-process); CS 22892-052 has [Ba/Fe]=+0.99 against [Eu/Fe]=+1.64, giving [Ba/Eu]=-0.65 (Eu >> Ba -- r-process). Same two elements, opposite sign of the ratio -- *that* flip, not either element's enhancement in isolation, is what actually tells you which process built the pattern.

Pb is the icing on the cake, and it's where the two curves' *shapes* diverge most dramatically (which is what Q12 asked you to look at): CS 29497-030 (CEMP-s) keeps climbing all the way to Pb -- [Ba/Fe]=+2.17 rises further to [Pb/Fe]=+3.55, more than a full 1 dex jump, making Pb by far its single most enhanced element. CS 22892-052 (CEMP-r) does the opposite: it peaks in the rare-earth/third r-process-peak region (Dy, Ir, Pt all around [X/Fe]~1.6-1.7) and turns back down by Pb ([Pb/Fe]=+1.20).

This Pb behaviour is the classic "lead star" signature of low-metallicity AGB s-process nucleosynthesis: at [Fe/H]=-2.70 there are very few Fe seeds available, so each seed that captures a neutron has, on average, far more free neutrons per seed to absorb than it would at solar metallicity. That pushes the s-process flow past the second peak (Ba-La-Ce) and piles material up disproportionately at the third peak (Pb) -- the opposite of solar metallicity, where there are enough Fe seeds that captures rarely get that far. The r-process pattern has no equivalent mechanism: it's set by a very different, explosive, neutron-rich site, and the scaled solar r-process-only residual simply doesn't carry as large a Pb/Bi peak relative to its own third peak (Pt-Os-Ir) as the low-metallicity s-process does -- so the r-process curve turns over near the rare earths/third peak instead of continuing to climb toward Pb.

**Q13.** Mass transfer dilutes the AGB companion's s-process-rich wind into the *unprocessed* envelope of the (much less evolved) low-mass star we actually observe -- which is why the discovery papers already model this as "a factor of ~2 dilution into an unprocessed envelope" rather than taking the donor's yields neat. A single-star model tells you what the *donor* produced internally, not what ends up mixed into the *observed* star's thin convective envelope, so the dilution factor (which depends on the accretor's own structure and mixing depth) is a free parameter no single-star yield table can give you. Binary evolution can also change the donor's own nucleosynthesis -- e.g. Roche-lobe overflow can truncate its AGB evolution early, cutting off the number of thermal pulses/dredge-up episodes it experiences -- so both "what s-process recipe was made" and "how much of it we actually see" depend on the binary history in ways single-star yields cannot capture.

**Q14.** The presence or absence of Tc (technetium) lines. Tc has no stable isotopes; its longest-lived, 99Tc, has a half-life of only ~2.1e5 years. If a CEMP-s star currently shows Tc lines, it must be *intrinsic* -- actively making s-process material via its own thermal pulses right now, since any transferred Tc would decay away long before the transfer-and-observe timescale. CS 29497-030 (like most CEMP-s stars, which are giants well below the AGB thermal-pulse phase) shows no Tc: it is *extrinsic*, carrying s-process material inherited from a companion AGB star that has already become a white dwarf -- exactly the scenario posed in Q13, and the standard intrinsic/extrinsic discriminant for any s-process-enhanced star.

This is also a historic result, not just a modern diagnostic: Paul Merrill first detected Tc lines in cool giant stars (S-type stars such as R Andromedae) in 1952. Since Tc has no stable isotope, its mere presence proved these giants were actively synthesising new heavy elements in their interiors *right now* -- the first direct observational evidence that stars really do manufacture elements, five years before Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler & Hoyle's 1957 theoretical synthesis of the whole picture (B2FH). It was a genuine milestone: nucleosynthesis inside stars went from theoretical prediction to an observed fact.
