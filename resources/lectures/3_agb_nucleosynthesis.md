# Nucleosynthesis in Asymptotic Giant Branch Stars

> [Slide 2]

Asymptotic Giant Branch stars are important sites for galactic chemical evolution for several reasons.

- **There are many of them:** The AGB is the evolutionary endpoint of a large fraction of stars in the Universe, so AGB stars make a significant cumulative contribution to the chemical enrichment of galaxies.
- **Low and intermediate mass stars have more time to process material:** Their longer evolutionary timescales allow nucleosynthesis to operate over extended periods, with material repeatedly processed and mixed before it is ultimately returned to the interstellar medium.
- **AGBs couple nucleosynthesis and mixing:** Nuclear burning and mixing occur in different regions of the star, allowing material produced in the interior to eventually reach the surface.
- **Low and intermediate mass stars experience their strongest mass loss during the AGB:** Their expanded envelopes are progressively removed through powerful stellar winds, returning processed material to the interstellar medium on relatively short timescales.
- **AGB nucleosynthesis is highly sensitive to stellar properties:** Initial mass and metallicity strongly influence the stellar structure, temperatures, mixing, neutron sources, and resulting abundance patterns. Surface abundances can therefore provide clues about the properties of individual stars and stellar populations.

This afternoon we will follow the journey of material through an AGB star: where it is produced, how it is transported to the surface, and how it is ultimately returned to the interstellar medium. Along the way, we'll see how changing mass and metallicity can move a star between very different nucleosynthetic regimes, and how we translate these stellar models into the yields used in chemical-evolution models.

> [Slide 3]

## What is an AGB star?

The AGB is one of the last major stages of nuclear burning in the evolution of low- and intermediate-mass stars ($\approx 1 - 8\, \rm M_\odot$; the exact mass range depends on metallicity).

Stars enter the early AGB after exhausting their central helium. They are left with a degenerate carbon–oxygen core that, despite continued contraction, never reaches the conditions required for central carbon ignition. Instead, helium burning shifts to a shell surrounding the C–O core, while hydrogen burning continues in a shell further out. 

> [Slide 4]

The star has expanded into a cool giant by this stage, with a large convective envelope surrounding the burning shells. The extended envelope is only weakly bound to the core, and stellar winds progressively erode it until the compact remnant is eventually exposed.


For sufficiently massive AGB stars, the helium-burning shell eventually becomes thermally unstable. This marks the transition from the early AGB to the thermally pulsing AGB (TP-AGB), characterised by periodic thermonuclear runaway events in the helium-burning shell.

## The Thermally Pulsing AGB Engine

- add figure: helium burning luminosity curves showing thermal pulse peaks. 

The TP-AGB is not a steady-burning phase. Instead, the star undergoes repeated cycles of **He-shell instability, thermal pulses, envelope expansion, dredge-up, and renewed shell burning**.

### The Early AGB

While the He-burning shell is established after core He exhaustion, the H-burning shell continues to deposit ash onto the layer above it, progressively increasing the pressure on the He-burning shell. As the He shell burns through this material and becomes geometrically thinner, it becomes increasingly susceptible to the thin-shell instability. The shell cannot expand sufficiently to provide the usual stabilising feedback—a drop in temperature—so the nuclear energy generation rate continues to rise. This eventually triggers a thermonuclear runaway, or thermal pulse.

### The Thermal Pulse 

As the He-burning shell becomes unstable, its temperature rises rapidly. The triple-$\alpha$ reaction is extremely temperature sensitive, so even a modest increase in temperature produces a sharp increase in the helium-burning rate and hence the energy generation. This rapid energy release drives a **pulse-driven convective zone** throughout the He-intershell, rapidly mixing and homogenising the material accumulated there. The energy released by the pulse also temporarily extinguishes the H-burning shell.

### Third Dredge-Up 

As the pulse subsides, the energy released by the He flash drives the envelope outward. The resulting expansion and cooling allow the convective envelope to move inwards into material processed during the pulse. Material from the He-intershell, including newly synthesised $^{12}\mathrm{C}$, $^{16}\mathrm{O}$, and products of neutron-capture nucleosynthesis, is then mixed into the envelope and transported to the stellar surface. The **third dredge-up** therefore provides the principal link between nucleosynthesis in the interior and the abundance patterns observed at the surface.

- **$^{13}\mathrm{C}$ pocket**: Following third dredge-up, partial mixing of protons into the $^{12}\mathrm{C}$-rich intershell can produce a $^{13}\mathrm{C}$-rich layer through the reaction $^{12}\mathrm{C}(p,\gamma)^{13}\mathrm{N} \rightarrow ^{13}\mathrm{C}$. This $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ pocket can subsequently act as a neutron source via $^{13}\mathrm{C}(\alpha,n)^{16}\mathrm{O}$. 

### Interpulse Phase

After the thermal pulse, the star returns to its quiescent configuration: the convective envelope retreats, the H-burning shell is re-established, and the star enters the interpulse phase. Hydrogen burns steadily, depositing fresh helium onto the intershell and increasing the pressure and temperature at its base until the thin-shell instability triggers the next thermal pulse.

- **Hot-Bottom Burning**: In sufficiently massive AGB stars, the base of the convective envelope becomes hot enough for hydrogen burning, allowing proton-capture reactions to alter the envelope composition. HBB activates the CNO, Ne--Na, and Mg--Al cycles, enhancing N, Na, and Al while preventing the star from becoming carbon-rich.

### Summary 

Importantly, the cycle evolves from pulse to pulse as the core and envelope masses, temperatures, composition, and mass-loss rate change. The number and strength of pulses, efficiency of third dredge-up, formation of a $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ pocket, and operation of HBB therefore depend on the initial conditions. 

Meanwhile, stellar winds progressively remove the envelope, so nucleosynthetic products are repeatedly processed, mixed, and ejected throughout the TP-AGB. The next question is how initial mass and metallicity determine which processes dominate.

## How Mass and Metallicity Shape AGB Nucleosynthesis

So we've now established that the AGB is a really rich site for nucleosynthesis. But that doesn't mean that all of these processes happen in all AGB stars. In fact, one of the most useful ways to think about AGB nucleosynthesis is to put the stars on a **mass–metallicity plane**. Where a star sits on this plane determines what happens to it. And the reason is that both mass and metallicity change the internal structure of the star — which changes the temperatures, densities, burning timescales, and mixing. 

> [Slide 9]

So let's start by taking a slice of this and moving up the mass axis.

> [Slide 10]

### Mass

> [Slide 11]

As the initial mass increases, the star develops a more massive and hotter core during its earlier evolution. That matters because the core mass at the end of helium burning largely determines what happens next. At the low-mass end, the star doesn't get hot enough to ignite carbon. It finishes core helium burning, develops the familiar C–O core surrounded by H- and He-burning shells, and eventually enters the thermally pulsing AGB. But there is a threshold. The core has to reach the right conditions for the star to actually enter the thermally pulsing phase. Below that threshold, the star can leave the AGB before it develops the full sequence of thermal pulses. So already, the initial mass is determining whether some of the most important AGB nucleosynthesis processes can happen at all.

> [Slide 12]

Once we move to somewhat higher masses, the star can undergo more thermal pulses, and third dredge-up becomes increasingly important. Remember what third dredge-up is. During a thermal pulse, the He shell becomes unstable and produces a convective region in the intershell. After the pulse, the envelope can move inward and mix some of that material to the surface. So now we have a mechanism for taking material that was produced deep inside the star and actually putting it into the envelope — where the star can eventually lose it through its wind. This is where the surface chemistry starts to change. At lower masses, repeated dredge-up can gradually increase the carbon abundance until the envelope becomes carbon rich. But as we increase the initial mass, things start to change. The intershell becomes smaller, so there is less material available to dredge up. At the same time, the envelope is more massive, so whatever material is dredged up is mixed into a larger reservoir. So simply having third dredge-up isn't enough. What matters is **how much material is dredged up relative to the envelope that receives it**.

> [Slide 13]

And then we introduce another process. At sufficiently high masses, the base of the convective envelope becomes hot enough for nuclear burning to occur directly there. This is **hot bottom burning**.  Instead of dredging carbon into the envelope and allowing it to accumulate, we can now take that carbon and process it through proton-capture reactions. So carbon that has been dredged up can be converted into nitrogen. And this is an important source of **primary nitrogen**: the carbon doesn't have to have been present in the star initially. The star makes the carbon through helium burning, dredges it into the envelope, and then processes it into nitrogen through HBB. As the temperature increases further, we can move through different proton-capture regimes. First the CN cycle becomes important. Then the full CNO cycle. And at still higher temperatures, the Ne–Na and Mg–Al chains become active. So as we move to higher mass, the surface chemistry changes from something that looks increasingly carbon rich to something that can become strongly nitrogen rich and, eventually, show the signatures of more advanced proton-capture burning.

The AGB is therefore not one nucleosynthesis site. It contains a sequence of different regimes controlled, in large part, by stellar mass. The models in our recent grid show exactly this competition between thermal pulses, dredge-up and HBB: third dredge-up enriches the envelope in carbon and heavy elements, while HBB can counteract that carbon enrichment and alter the CNO, Ne–Na and Mg–Al abundances. And if we keep increasing the mass, we eventually reach another boundary. We're now getting hot enough to ignite carbon. For stars in the super-AGB regime, carbon ignition occurs before the star has enough mass to proceed through the usual massive-star path to an iron core. So now we have a competition between **core growth and mass loss and dredge-up**. If the core continues to grow towards the Chandrasekhar mass, the star can ultimately undergo an electron-capture supernova. If it loses enough of its envelope, and core growth is halted, it can instead leave behind an oxygen–neon white dwarf. So even the endpoint of the star is sitting somewhere on this mass axis.

### Metallicity

So what happens if we take the same mass and change the metallicity? This is where things get really interesting. I'm going to split the diagram into two broad regimes: **[Fe/H] ≳ −2.2** and **[Fe/H] ≲ −2.2.**. 

I don't want you to think of −2.2 as a fundamental boundary in nature. It's a useful division for the behaviour we're discussing here. The transitions are gradual, and they depend on the model and the particular process we're looking at. 

> [Slide 16]

Let's start with the more familiar, relatively metal-rich side.

### [Fe/H] ≳ −2.2

> [Slide 17]

Metallicity changes the structure of the star before it even reaches the AGB. One of the important effects is opacity. At higher metallicity, the stellar material has more sources of opacity. The envelope therefore becomes more extended and less dense, and the star tends to have a cooler interior than its metal-poor counterpart at the same mass.There is another effect as well: the mean molecular weight increases with metallicity. So the two effects push in opposite directions. At around solar metallicity, the opacity effect is important. At sufficiently high metallicity, the increase in mean molecular weight starts to become more important and the stellar structure changes again. This is one reason we should be careful about saying that metallicity simply makes a star “hotter” or “cooler”. The actual stellar structure is the result of several competing effects. But across the AGB regime we're interested in, the important result is that metallicity changes the conditions under which the star experiences thermal pulses, dredge-up and HBB. And we can see this very clearly in the models.

> [Slide 18]

### Thermal pulses

At lower metallicity, the models generally develop **stronger thermal pulses**. As we increase metallicity, the pulses become weaker and the overall TP-AGB evolution changes. And this then feeds directly into third dredge-up. A stronger thermal pulse produces a stronger expansion of the layers above the He shell, allowing the envelope to penetrate further into the intershell. 

> [Slide 19]

### Third Dredge Up

So stronger pulses generally mean more efficient dredge-up. And therefore: **lower metallicity → stronger pulses → more efficient TDU → more material from the intershell reaches the surface.** Conversely: **higher metallicity → weaker pulses → less efficient TDU.** This also means that the minimum mass required for efficient third dredge-up moves upwards as metallicity increases.

So in a metal-rich population, fewer stars are able to become strongly enriched by AGB dredge-up. And this gives us an interesting population-level consequence. At low metallicity, you don't need to add very much carbon to the envelope before you get C/O > 1, because there is less oxygen there to begin with. At high metallicity, there is simply more oxygen in the initial envelope. So you need to dredge up much more carbon to become a carbon star. But at the same time, third dredge-up is becoming less efficient. So we get a kind of **double effect**: lower metallicity makes it easier to become carbon rich, while also making the dredge-up more efficient. Higher metallicity does the opposite. This is why the fraction of carbon-rich AGB stars is expected to change strongly with metallicity.

> [Slide 20]

### HBB

Now let's move back up the mass axis. At higher masses, we have HBB. And again, metallicity changes the temperature at the base of the convective envelope. The lower-metallicity stars are more compact and reach higher temperatures. So HBB can switch on at lower initial masses at lower metallicity. And, for a given mass, the lower-metallicity star can reach a more advanced HBB regime. So instead of simply saying: **"this star has HBB"** we should really ask: **"how hot does the base of the envelope get?"** Because the nucleosynthesis changes with temperature. At one temperature, carbon is converted into nitrogen through the CN cycle. At higher temperatures, the full CNO cycle operates. At still higher temperatures, we start activating the Ne–Na and Mg–Al chains. So metallicity is effectively moving the boundaries between these different nucleosynthetic regimes. And this is why two stars with the same initial mass can have very different surface compositions if they formed at different metallicities.

> [Slide 21]

### Very high metallicity

And then, at the other extreme, we get to the really metal-rich stars. Here the picture becomes slightly less intuitive. As metallicity becomes very high, the increasing mean molecular weight begins to compete strongly with the opacity effect. The models actually become hotter and more luminous again at the highest metallicities. But this does **not** restore the usual AGB behaviour. The important result for the AGB is that third dredge-up remains strongly suppressed, and the temperatures at the base of the envelope do not recover the efficiency of HBB that we see at lower metallicity. So you can't just extrapolate from solar metallicity and assume that the trends continue linearly. The very metal-rich models can behave qualitatively differently. In the earlier super-solar calculations, for example, only a small subset of the highest-metallicity models even reached the TP-AGB, and the models that did reach it showed little or no third dredge-up. And that's an important lesson in stellar modelling: **you can't necessarily extrapolate the behaviour of a solar-metallicity star into a completely different metallicity regime.**

### [Fe/H] ≲ −2.2

Now let's go to the other side of the diagram. At sufficiently low metallicity, something new appears. We're no longer just changing the efficiency of the usual AGB processes. We're changing the evolutionary pathway itself. This is where we start seeing **proton ingestion events** and the associated dual-flash behaviour. 

> [Slide 24]

At extremely low metallicity, there are very few CNO nuclei in the envelope. That changes how hydrogen burning operates. In some of these models, the He-burning convection zone can interact with the H-rich layers. Protons are mixed down into hot, carbon-rich material. They encounter freshly produced carbon, and you can get a burst of CNO burning. This produces what Campbell referred to as **dual shell flashes** on the AGB. And these events can pollute the envelope before the star has undergone the normal sequence of efficient third dredge-up episodes. For example, in Campbell's models, the dual shell flash mixes protons downward and CNO-rich material upward. The processed material is subsequently dredged into the envelope, producing a substantial change in the surface composition. This is particularly interesting because it gives us a way of making **very carbon-rich stars at extremely low iron abundance**. 

And it gives us something else that is slightly counter-intuitive. These stars can also become very nitrogen rich. Because once carbon has been produced and mixed into the envelope, the high temperatures of the low-metallicity star can process that carbon through HBB and convert it into nitrogen. So at low metallicity, you can get both: **very carbon-rich low-mass stars** and **very nitrogen-rich stars at surprisingly low masses.** This is one of the reasons the mass–metallicity plane is so useful. The same process that looks like a small correction at solar metallicity can become the dominant source of the surface composition at extremely low metallicity. Campbell's models illustrate this particularly nicely. In some of the lowest-metallicity models, the surface composition becomes dominated by material produced during the dual-flash events rather than by normal AGB dredge-up. 

> [Slide 25]

### And what does this do to the s-process? / i-process

Now we can bring in something we talked about earlier in the lecture. The s-process depends not just on the neutron source, but also on the **number of seed nuclei available to capture those neutrons**. For the low-mass AGB stars we're interested in, the main neutron source is the 13C(α,n)16O reaction. This is a useful distinction. The neutron source can be relatively primary — but the seeds are not. So imagine two stars producing roughly the same number of neutrons. The metal-poor star has fewer iron-group seed nuclei. That means each seed nucleus can experience more neutron captures. So the neutron exposure per seed can be much larger. And that pushes the nucleosynthesis towards heavier s-process nuclei. This is why metal-poor AGB stars are particularly efficient producers of the heavy s-process elements. You can think of it as: **same neutron supply + fewer seeds = more neutrons available per seed.** And that changes where the material accumulates. At relatively high metallicity, we tend to favour production around the first s-process peak. As we decrease the metallicity, the distribution shifts towards the second and eventually the third peak — including Pb.

But eventually we get so metal poor that the usual picture starts to break down. At essentially zero metallicity, there are almost no Fe seeds to begin with. And if you also don't have efficient third dredge-up to establish the usual 13C-pocket neutron source, the standard AGB s-process becomes much harder to operate. This is where the proton-ingestion and i-process possibilities become interesting. Campbell's calculations found that proton-ingestion events can generate substantial neutron production through 13C(α,n)16O. The original calculations could not follow the full s-process because of the limitations of the nuclear network, but they identified the possibility that these events could contribute to neutron-capture nucleosynthesis in the early Universe. So at the extreme metal-poor end, we're no longer simply turning the strength of the ordinary s-process up and down. We're potentially accessing **different neutron-capture regimes altogether**.

### So what does the whole plane look like?

> [Slide 28]

If we put everything back together, we can start to sketch out the AGB mass–metallicity plane. At low mass and relatively high metallicity, we have relatively cool AGB stars where third dredge-up can make them carbon rich. Move to higher mass and HBB becomes important, converting carbon into nitrogen and activating progressively hotter proton-capture networks. Move to still higher mass and we approach the super-AGB regime, where carbon ignition and the competition between core growth and mass loss determine the eventual fate. Now move horizontally towards lower metallicity. The stars become hotter and more compact. Thermal pulses become stronger. Third dredge-up becomes more efficient. HBB switches on at lower masses and reaches higher temperatures. And the s-process becomes more efficient at producing the heavier peaks because there are fewer seed nuclei. And then, at the extreme metal-poor end, proton ingestion and dual-flash events introduce additional nucleosynthetic pathways.

Every feature in the yield is telling you something about **where the star sits on the mass–metallicity plane**.

### From individual stars to populations

So far, we've been talking about individual stars. But a galaxy doesn't contain one 2-solar-mass star. It contains a lot of 2-solar-mass stars, fewer 5-solar-mass stars, and many fewer 8-solar-mass stars. The distribution of stellar masses is described by the **initial mass function**, or IMF. So if we want to know what AGB stars contribute to a galaxy, we can't just take the yield from whichever mass produces the most interesting nucleosynthesis. We have to weight the individual stellar yields by how many stars of each mass actually exist. For a Salpeter IMF, for example, the number of stars decreases approximately as a power law with increasing mass. Cinquegrana & Karakas used this weighting to construct population yields, integrating the individual stellar yields across the AGB mass range. And this has a very important consequence. A rare 7- or 8-solar-mass star can have an enormous yield of some element. But if there are hundreds of lower-mass stars for every one of those stars, that individual yield may not dominate the **population yield**. The population yield is therefore a competition between: **how much a star produces** and **how many stars like that actually exist.**
