# An introduction to stellar yields [15 minutes]

## What are yields

So we've just spent the last part of the lecture talking about where nucleosynthesis happens. But if I want to actually use a stellar model to understand the chemical evolution of a galaxy, I need to turn all of that information inside the star into something that I can actually use. And that's where **stellar yields** come in. A stellar yield is essentially the amount of a particular element or isotope that a star produces and eventually returns to its surroundings. So rather than saying, *this star has a temperature of this, a density of this, and this abundance profile*, we can summarise the result as something like:

> a 3 solar-mass star at this metallicity produces this much carbon, this much nitrogen, this much fluorine, this much of each of the s-process elements...

And we can do the same thing for a massive star, or a supernova, or a population of stars. So a yield table is basically a way of taking the output of a stellar evolution and nucleosynthesis calculation and making it usable for the next level of modelling. And this is really important because, as we talked about in the introduction, we can't put every individual star in a galaxy into a detailed stellar evolution calculation. Instead, we calculate grids of stellar models. For example, we might have models from 1 to 8 solar masses, at a range of metallicities, and calculate the nucleosynthetic yields for each one. Then a galactic chemical-evolution model can take those yields and ask: *given the population of stars that formed, how much of each element gets returned to the interstellar medium?* So the yield is the link between **stellar evolution and galactic chemical evolution**.

And there are a couple of slightly different ways we can define a yield. We can talk about the total mass of an isotope that is ejected, or we can talk about the **net yield** — the amount that the star has produced in excess of what it started with. That distinction matters. If a star starts with a large amount of, say, oxygen, and eventually ejects that oxygen, it doesn't necessarily mean the star *produced* all of that oxygen. Some of it was already there when the star was born. So when we're interested in nucleosynthesis, we're often interested in the **newly synthesised material** rather than simply the total amount that gets ejected. And even this apparently simple quantity depends on what we decide counts as ejecta. Which brings us to the next question.

## What goes into them

A yield isn't something that comes directly out of the nuclear reaction network. There are actually several layers of physics between the nuclear reactions and the final number in a yield table. The first is the **initial stellar model**. We need to specify things like the initial mass and metallicity — essentially, what kind of star are we starting with? Then we need to evolve that star. And this is where things like convection, mixing, mass loss, rotation, and the treatment of convective boundaries become important. All of these can change where material is transported inside the star, how long different burning phases last, and which layers eventually get exposed and ejected.

For example, in an AGB star, the amount of material that gets dredged up from the intershell into the envelope can have a huge effect on the final carbon and neutron-capture yields. And the amount of mass loss determines how long the star remains on the AGB, which affects how many thermal pulses and dredge-up episodes it experiences. So the yield isn't just telling us about nuclear physics. It's also telling us about our **stellar evolution assumptions**. And this is one of the reasons yield calculations have uncertainties.

In my own work, for example, one of the major uncertainties in AGB yields is the mass-loss prescription. Increasing the mass-loss rate can shorten the AGB lifetime and reduce the number of thermal pulses, while lower mass loss can allow the star to experience more pulses and potentially more dredge-up. There are similar issues in massive stars. You have the evolution of the star, the composition of the different shells, and then, when the star explodes, you have to decide what actually gets ejected.

For a core-collapse supernova, there is a particularly important concept called the **mass cut**. This is essentially the boundary between the material that becomes part of the compact remnant and the material that gets ejected. And that matters enormously for the yields, particularly for the innermost, iron-group material. In many one-dimensional explosion calculations, the explosion itself is not obtained self-consistently. Instead, an explosion is triggered using something like a piston or an energy deposition, and parameters such as the explosion energy and mass cut are specified or calibrated. So if I change that assumption, I can change the predicted yield.

And that's something worth keeping in mind when you look at a yield table. A yield isn't an observation. It's a **model prediction**. It is the result of a chain of assumptions about the initial star, its evolution, its nuclear physics, how material is mixed, how mass is lost, and, for an exploding star, how the explosion is treated. And then there's another big question: **how good is our approximation of the star itself?** Which brings us to the 1D versus 3D problem.

## 1D vs 3D

- islands of explodability shift. 

Almost all of the stellar evolution models that we use to produce large grids of stellar yields are **one-dimensional**. And at first that sounds a little strange. Stars are obviously three-dimensional objects. Convection is three-dimensional. Rotation is three-dimensional. Instabilities are three-dimensional. Explosions are three-dimensional.

So why are we modelling them in one dimension? The answer is basically computational feasibility. A 1D stellar evolution model assumes that the star can be represented as a series of spherical shells. So instead of following the full three-dimensional flow of gas, we track quantities like temperature, density and composition as a function of radius — or, more commonly, enclosed mass. This is incredibly useful because it lets us evolve an entire star through millions or billions of years at a computational cost that is manageable. And that's why we can calculate **large grids of stellar models**.

But we have to replace the physics that we can't resolve with prescriptions. Convection is a really good example. In reality, convection involves turbulent three-dimensional flows. In a 1D model, we can't resolve those flows, so we use a prescription to decide where convection occurs and how efficiently material is mixed. And even relatively small differences in those prescriptions can change the structure of the star and therefore its nucleosynthesis. For example, different treatments of convective boundaries, overshooting and semiconvection can produce different convective core masses. Three-dimensional simulations can also show behaviour such as entrainment at convective boundaries that isn't naturally captured by the standard 1D prescriptions. So there's a fundamental trade-off.

**1D models are computationally cheap enough to evolve whole stars and produce large yield grids, but they have to approximate inherently multidimensional physics.** Three-dimensional simulations can resolve much more of that physics. But they're enormously more expensive. So you generally can't just take a 3D simulation and evolve an entire star from the zero-age main sequence all the way through its death in the same way that you can with a 1D stellar evolution code. Instead, 3D simulations are often used to study particular physical problems in detail — convection, mixing, instabilities, burning fronts, explosions — and then we try to understand how those results should inform the prescriptions used in 1D models. 

And this is one of the big challenges in stellar astrophysics. We need the 1D models because they're the only practical way to explore huge regions of parameter space. But the things that we're approximating in 1D are often exactly the things that have a large influence on the nucleosynthesis. So when you see a yield table, you shouldn't think of it as: **"this is how much carbon a 3-solar-mass star produces."** You should really think: **"this is how much carbon this particular model predicts, given its assumptions about stellar evolution, nuclear physics, mixing, mass loss, and — where relevant — the explosion."**
